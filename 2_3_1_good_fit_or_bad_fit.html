Understand MTS and MTO
Decoupling-point fit

Is this decision a good fit for the business?

Different products need different supply-chain responses. Standard power tools often rely on make-to-stock, while fitted kitchens are typically make-to-order.

Make to Stock (MTS)

Finished products are produced before the customer order. This supports fast availability but creates forecast and inventory risk.

Make to Order (MTO)

Production begins after the customer order and specifications are known. This supports customization but usually requires a longer lead time.

Key takeaway

The best decoupling point depends on the business.

A choice is favourable only when it fits customer expectations, product variety, demand predictability, lead-time tolerance, and inventory cost.

🔧
MTS fits standard products

Customers expect immediate availability. Finished products can be produced from forecasts and held close to the market. The main risk is excess or obsolete inventory.

🛠️
MTO fits customized products

Customer specifications must be known before production. This reduces the risk of making the wrong product, but customers usually accept a longer lead time.

📦
Forecast risk matters

Downstream decoupling increases speed but also increases exposure to overstock and obsolescence when demand is misjudged.

There is no universally best position. Moving the decoupling point downstream supports speed but requires more forecast-based inventory. Moving it upstream supports customization but creates longer order-driven lead times.
